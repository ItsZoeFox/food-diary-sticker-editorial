# Food Background Routing

Use this reference whenever the surrounding table scene is cleaned or rebuilt. Diversity must come from food-specific art direction, not random props.

## Selection Sequence

1. Preserve scene truth first. Keep restaurant, street-food, handheld, or shared-meal context when it carries the memory.
2. Profile the dish before choosing a background:
   - dominant lightness: pale, medium, or dark;
   - dominant hue and one accent hue;
   - appetizing cue: creamy, clear, crisp, glossy, fresh, caramelized, or colorful;
   - vessel color, material, and outline;
   - confident cuisine or occasion cues, if any.
3. Choose exactly one primary surface family that creates separation from both food and vessel without fighting them.
4. Choose one lighting motif and at most one or two quiet props.
5. Match camera angle, scale, focus falloff, light direction, color temperature, and contact shadow to the locked photographic dish.

## Surface Families

- **Honed stone / travertine / limestone:** use for creamy, pale, delicate, mushroom, herb, or softly browned food. Choose a tone darker or warmer than the sauce so pale food stays legible. Keep pores subtle and clean.
- **Fine terrazzo:** use for colorful diced ingredients, salads, brunch, playful home meals, or graphic vessels. Use a light neutral ground with sparse chips sampled from the food; never let the chips resemble crumbs.
- **Glazed tile / terracotta tile:** use for green vegetables, braises, red chilies, rustic-but-clean home cooking, or warm regional moods. Keep grout narrow and spotless. Use complementary clay tones for green food and cooler tiles for orange-red food.
- **Lacquer tray / smooth dark surface:** use for pale rice, light soups, tofu, steamed food, or restrained East Asian service. Avoid black voids, mirror glare, and costume-like cultural decoration.
- **Frosted glass / brushed metal / pale composite:** use for modern, clean, chilled, crisp, or minimal dishes. Maintain soft reflections and avoid clinical laboratory styling.
- **Fine textile / paper mat:** use for desserts, tea, breakfast, or soft homemade food. Prefer fine weave, subtle checks, delicate cotton, or paper texture. Coarse beige linen is not the universal default.
- **Wood:** use only when warmth, rustic cooking, breakfast, bread, grilling, or the original home context makes it the strongest choice. Vary species, tone, finish, and layout; do not automatically add beige linen.

## Contrast Routing

- Pale or creamy food: use medium-value stone, muted cool tile, or dark smooth lacquer; avoid pale beige-on-beige scenes.
- Highly colorful food: use quiet light terrazzo, pale composite, or restrained neutral tile; let the dish carry the saturation.
- Brown plus green savory food: use warm complementary terracotta, dusty rose tile, or cool gray stone; avoid muddy brown wood.
- Clear soup or glossy sauce: use matte surfaces with controlled reflections.
- Crisp or fried food: use smooth darker surfaces or cool stone to separate golden edges without increasing sharpening.
- Dessert: use pale stone, frosted glass, fine textile, or café-like composite surfaces matched to fruit, caramel, chocolate, or cream.

## Batch Diversity

For two or more images in one request:

- map each image to a different primary surface family when plausible;
- do not repeat both the same surface and the same lighting motif;
- rotate among soft side-window light, geometric window shadow, overhead diffuse light, gentle evening practical light, and restaurant ambient light as the original illumination permits;
- vary one minor prop category only: cup, spoon, chopstick rest, small condiment dish, flower stem, or folded fine napkin;
- keep a shared editorial restraint so the batch feels related even when materials differ.

If recent outputs are visible, treat their primary background materials as temporarily used. Prefer an unused family unless fidelity or cuisine context makes repetition clearly better.

## Prop Limits

- Use zero to two props, placed outside the food's focal zone and often partially cropped or softly out of focus.
- Never add a prop merely to prove a cuisine or region.
- Remove computers, cables, boxes, branded packaging, bags, empty dishes, and unrelated work objects.
- Avoid food ingredients scattered on the table, fake crumbs, loose herbs, sauce splashes, or decorative clutter.

## Coherence Gates

Reject or revise the background when any of these occur:

- the food looks pasted onto a different layer;
- the vessel and surface have incompatible perspective or contact shadow;
- the background shares nearly the same hue and value as both vessel and food;
- texture competes with the food or resembles dirt, crumbs, grease, or sauce;
- props become more noticeable than the dish or sticker;
- the setting reads as a stereotype, a commercial studio, or a pristine showroom rather than a believable meal;
- a batch repeats wood grain plus coarse linen without a specific reason.
